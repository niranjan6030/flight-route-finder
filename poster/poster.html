Flight Route Finder

Finding connecting flights between any two airports with Dijkstra's algorithm and A* search
Niranjan S
BCA, CHRIST (Deemed to be University), Bengaluru
October 2026

1. The routing problem

Big cities have direct flights to each other. Small airports do not. In my data Mysuru has flights only to Bengaluru, and Shillong only to Kolkata. Somebody going from Mysuru to Shillong has to change planes, and has to work out where to change.

Problem: given a starting airport and a destination, find the chain of flights that is best for the passenger. "Best" can mean reaching quickest, taking the fewest flights or flying the shortest distance. If an airport is closed (fog, a cyclone) or the passenger cannot pass through it (no transit visa), the route must go round it.

As a graph: every airport is a node, every flight is a directed edge with a cost, and I need the cheapest path from one node to another (single-pair shortest path).

Mysuru to Shillong in my prototype: 3 flights, changing at Bengaluru and Kolkata.

2. Inputs and outputs

Input • From and To airport (type a city, airport name or 3-letter code, or click a dot on the map).
• What matters most: quickest, fewest flights or shortest distance.
• Hours wanted between two flights (1 to 4), and airports to avoid.
• The network: 3,257 airports with latitude and longitude, 37,042 routes.
Output • The flights to take in order, with km and time of each, and the total.
• Where to change planes, and up to 3 other routes to choose from.
• The route on a world map and a link to check each flight on Google Flights.
• How many airports the search looked at.

Data: OpenFlights open database. I cleaned it with a Python script: removed airports without a code, kept every route once (67,663 rows became 37,042) and corrected 20 Indian city names (Madras to Chennai, Patina to Patna). 225 countries, 71 airports in India. 820 routes have no flight back, so the graph is directed.

3. The prototype

Hubballi to Leh: the answer with 3 other routes on the left, the map on the right.

A web page in HTML and plain JavaScript with Bootstrap and Leaflet. Nothing to install, works on a phone. It draws the world map itself from land outline data, so it works without internet.

  • Next to every change of planes there is "avoid this airport". One click and it finds a way round.
  • A "How was this found?" box draws the airports that Dijkstra and A* looked at.

Files: index.html, js/app.js (the page), js/routing.js (all the algorithms, 400 lines), tools/build_data.py (cleans the data), tests.html, benchmark.html.

4. The algorithm

The cost of one flight

Every flight costs a × km + b, where km is the great circle distance (haversine formula). Only a and b change, so one search function does all three kinds of "best". No cost is negative.

Best meansabThe total is in
Shortest distance10km
Quickest1/8000.5 + waithours (800 km/h + 0.5 h a flight)
Fewest flights0.0000011flights (km only breaks ties)

Dijkstra's algorithm

  1. The start gets cost 0, every other airport infinity. Put the start in a min-heap.
  2. Take the airport with the smallest cost out of the heap. Its cost is now final ("finished").
  3. For every flight out of it: if this way is cheaper than what the next airport has now, write the new cost, note where it came from and put it in the heap.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until the destination is finished. Walk back through the notes to get the route.

A*: the same thing with a guess

In A* the heap is ordered by cost so far + a guess of the cost still to go. My guess is the cost of one direct flight to the destination. No real route can be cheaper than that, so the guess is never too high and the answer stays the best one. Airports in the wrong direction get a big guess and sink in the heap.

Pseudocode

best[start] = 0, best[all others] = infinity
heap.push(start, guess(start))
while heap is not empty
    u = heap.pop()              // smallest cost + guess
    if u is finished, skip it   // old copy
    mark u finished
    if u is the destination, stop
    for every flight u -> v, v not finished or closed
        c = best[u] + a * km(u, v) + b
        if c < best[v]
            best[v] = c;  cameFrom[v] = u
            heap.push(v, c + guess(v))
guess(v) = a * straightLineKm(v, destination) + b
           (guess = 0 gives plain Dijkstra)

5. Why this algorithm?

I wrote five methods and timed them on the same 100 random trips ("quickest" cost, full network). ms = milliseconds for one search.

MethodBest route?TimemsWhat I found
Breadth first searchFewest flights onlyO(V + E)0.14Does not look at km or hours. Not the quickest in 72 of 100 trips.
Dijkstra without a heapYesO(V²)13.15Looks through all airports to find the next one.
Bellman-FordYesO(V · E)1.09Made for negative costs, which I do not have. Cannot stop early.
Floyd-WarshallYes, all pairsO(V³)not tried3.5 × 1010 steps and a table of 10.6 million numbers to answer one question.
Dijkstra with a min-heapYesO((V + E) log V)0.6321 times faster than without the heap.
A* (chosen)Yessame, worst case0.28Same answers. Finishes about 1 airport in 6.

Why it fits this problem: costs are never negative. I need one start and one destination, not all pairs. The network is sparse (11 routes per airport on average, 0.35% of all possible pairs), so a heap pays off. Every airport has a position, so the straight line guess is free. And the answer must come while the user waits.

Dijkstra: 1,853 airports finished
A*: 12 airports finished
Kochi to Toronto, same answer from both (change at Dubai). Dijkstra spreads in every direction, A* goes towards the destination. This trip is a good case, the average is in section 8.

V = 3,257 airports, E = 37,042 routes. A V × V table would need 10.6 million cells, the lists need 37,042. The heap can grow to E in the worst case (I push a new copy instead of changing the old one). Measured: biggest heap 1,028 items on average for Dijkstra, 862 for A*.

  • The heap matters most. At 3,257 airports Dijkstra takes 11.8 ms without it and 0.65 ms with it. From 200 to 3,257 airports the first got 98 times slower, the second 8 times.
  • A* finishes 12 to 27% of the airports Dijkstra does. It helps least for fewest flights, where the guess is almost the same (1 flight) for every airport.
  • But it is only about 2 times faster, not 6, because every guess needs the distance formula (sin, cos).
  • Bellman-Ford needed about 6 passes, not 3,256, but looked at 213,010 flights. Dijkstra looked at 12,971.

9. Who can use it, and limits

  • People in small towns who need to know where to change (Hubballi to Leh).
  • A hub is shut by fog or a cyclone: click "avoid" and see the next best way.
  • People who cannot pass through a country without a transit visa.

Different from a booking site: not tied to one airline, lets you rule out airports, shows how the answer was found.

Limits: the routes are from 2014, so some flights do not run now and new ones are missing. No timetable and no ticket price, the wait is the same at every airport. Next: real schedules, so that the cost becomes the real arrival time, and price as a fourth kind of cost.
